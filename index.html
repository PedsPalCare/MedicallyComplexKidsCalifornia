For California families of children with complex medical needs

Five programs. No map. Until now.

In North Carolina, one waiver is the front door. In California, care for a medically fragile child runs through five separate systems — Medi-Cal, Regional Centers, CCS, IHSS, and the HCBA Waiver — each with its own application, its own rules, and its own waitlist. Families are expected to find them all on their own. We built the map instead.

Why we exist

Every medically fragile child in California deserves the chance to live — and thrive — at home.

We believe that every California child with a medically fragile condition — whatever their diagnosis, background, income, or ZIP code — should have access to the healthcare, home nursing, education, and family support that makes life at home possible. The programs exist. The barrier is finding them. This site is written by clinicians and family advocates who navigate these systems every day, in plain language, with the deadlines and fine print families actually need.

The roadmap: what to apply for, in what order

If your child was just diagnosed, just discharged, or you just moved to California — this page is the map. Apply for these programs in roughly this order. Most can (and should) run at the same time; none of them require finishing the others first.

Program details last reviewed July 2026 — always confirm with the agency
The single most important thing to know

Unlike North Carolina's CAP/C, California has no single waiver that unlocks everything. Each program below is a separate application to a separate agency. No one will enroll you automatically, and no agency will tell you about the others. Families who get full services are the families who applied to all five.

  1. Apply for Medi-Cal — even if you think you earn too much

    Medi-Cal is the foundation: IHSS, the DD Waiver, and most CCS benefits require it. Children qualify at family incomes up to 266% of the federal poverty level — and children with high medical needs can qualify regardless of parent income through "institutional deeming" on a waiver (step 2 and step 5). If you're denied on income now, the waivers can fix that later, so don't stop here.

    Read the Medi-Cal guide → · Apply at BenefitsCal.com or your county social services office.

  2. Call your Regional Center

    If your child has (or may have) a developmental disability or delay — including cerebral palsy, epilepsy, intellectual disability, autism, or conditions requiring similar support — California's Lanterman Act entitles them to Regional Center services for life, at no cost, with no waitlist. Babies 0–3 go through Early Start, where "at risk of delay" is enough. The Regional Center is also a route to Medi-Cal regardless of parent income, via the DD Waiver.

    Read the Regional Center guide →

  3. Ask for a CCS referral

    California Children's Services pays for specialists, hospital care, equipment, and therapy related to an eligible condition up to age 21 — and runs the Medical Therapy Program (PT/OT) in public schools with no income test at all. Any doctor, hospital, or you yourself can refer. If your child sees pediatric specialists for a serious condition, they probably qualify.

    Read the CCS guide →

  4. Apply for IHSS once Medi-Cal is active

    In-Home Supportive Services pays a caregiver — including you, the parent — by the hour for the extra care your child needs beyond ordinary parenting: paramedical tasks, personal care, protective supervision. Since February 2024 there are no more restrictions on parents being paid providers. Apply through your county IHSS office; expect a home assessment.

    Read the IHSS guide →

  5. Get on the HCBA Waiver list — today, not later

    The Home and Community-Based Alternatives Waiver is California's closest cousin to CAP/C: nurse + social worker care management, home nursing, and Medi-Cal based on the child's income alone. It has a waitlist (since July 2023), but children under 21 are a priority group. The application is a six-page form — send it in even while everything else is pending. If your child is trach/vent dependent, tube fed, or nursing-level complex, this is the program that makes home sustainable, and adults over 21 can't get private duty nursing any other way.

    Read the HCBA Waiver guide →

  6. Then: the safety net around the programs

    • Backup power plan — Medical Baseline, battery programs, and outage registries before fire season.
    • Community resources — Family Resource Centers, parent groups, palliative care, and legal help.
    • The 18–21 transition — if your child is 16+, start this now; several deadlines are unforgiving.
Family tip: the binder (or the folder on your phone)

Every one of these applications wants the same things: diagnosis list, medication list, clinic notes, and a letter from your child's doctor describing daily care needs. Ask your pediatrician or specialty clinic for one strong "letter of medical necessity" describing everything your child needs in a 24-hour day — then reuse it everywhere. Keep copies of every form you submit and note the date and the name of everyone you talk to.

If you're overwhelmed

You don't have to do this alone. Every county has a federally funded Family Resource Center staffed by parents of children with disabilities who will help you fill out these exact forms for free — and hospital social workers, Regional Center service coordinators, and CCS case managers can each carry part of this. About us

Our mission & vision

We are dedicated to advocating for the rights and needs of medically fragile children and their families across California.

We believe that every child in California who has a medically fragile condition — whatever their background, diagnosis, or circumstances — should have the opportunity to live their best life possible, and should have access to the healthcare, home nursing, education, and other supports needed to make this a reality.

Why this site exists

This project was inspired by Advocates for Medically Fragile Kids NC, a volunteer-run organization whose plain-language guides to North Carolina's CAP/C waiver have helped thousands of families. California has more medically fragile children than any state in the country — and no equivalent resource. Our programs are scattered across five agencies, our waiver has a waitlist, and the information families need lives in policy letters written for bureaucrats, not parents at a hospital bedside.

We translate those systems into steps a family can actually take. Our content is written and reviewed by pediatric complex care and palliative care clinicians and by parents who have navigated these programs themselves.

Who we serve

Children and young adults in California with complex, chronic, or life-limiting medical conditions — kids with tracheostomies and ventilators, feeding tubes, severe neurological impairment, genetic and metabolic conditions, and technology-dependence of every kind — and the parents, grandparents, foster families, and caregivers who love them. We also write for the professionals around them: social workers, discharge planners, nurses, therapists, and physicians who want to connect families to services.

Programs · Step 1

Medi-Cal for medically fragile kids

Medi-Cal is California's Medicaid program — and for children with complex medical needs, it's far more than insurance. It's the eligibility key to IHSS, waivers, and CCS, and under federal EPSDT rules it must cover what your child medically needs, even when a private plan won't.

Last reviewed July 2026 — confirm current rules with DHCS or your county
Who qualifies Income too high? EPSDT Home nursing Equipment & supplies Care coordination & hospice

Who qualifies

Children qualify for no-cost Medi-Cal at family incomes up to 266% of the federal poverty level — a much higher threshold than for adults. Children qualify regardless of immigration status. If your child has private insurance through a parent's job, they can still have Medi-Cal as secondary coverage; Medi-Cal then picks up copays, supplies, nursing, and services the private plan denies.

Apply online at BenefitsCal.com, through Covered California, or in person at your county social services office. Renewals happen yearly — never ignore a yellow renewal packet, because a missed renewal cancels everything built on top of Medi-Cal (IHSS hours stop, waiver services pause).

"We earn too much" — institutional deeming

Key fact

For most families, Medi-Cal counts parent income. But when a child is enrolled in the HCBA Waiver or the Regional Center's DD Waiver, the rules are waived — only the child's own income and assets count. This is called institutional deeming, and it's how middle- and higher-income California families get Medi-Cal for a medically fragile child.

Practically, this means: if you were denied Medi-Cal for income, your path is a waiver. For a child with a developmental disability, ask the Regional Center about DD Waiver enrollment for institutional deeming (no waitlist). For a child with nursing-level medical needs, apply to the HCBA Waiver (waitlisted, but children are prioritized). Once the waiver is active, apply for Medi-Cal again and tell the county the child is institutionally deemed.

EPSDT: the under-21 superpower

Federal law gives every Medi-Cal member under 21 a benefit called EPSDT (Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic and Treatment). In plain language: Medi-Cal must cover any medically necessary service that could correct or improve your child's condition — even if that service is limited or excluded for adults, and even if it isn't listed in the regular benefits handbook.

  • More therapy visits than the standard limit? Covered if medically necessary.
  • Private duty nursing at home? Covered under EPSDT for children who need it.
  • Diapers/incontinence supplies after the developmental age they'd normally be expected to toilet train? Covered.

When a service is denied, the magic words in your appeal are: "My child is under 21. Under EPSDT, Medi-Cal must cover this service because it is medically necessary to correct or ameliorate her condition." Ask the prescribing provider to use the same language.

Home nursing (private duty nursing)

Children who need skilled nursing care at home — trach/vent care, complex seizure management, continuous feeds with aspiration risk — can receive private duty nursing (PDN) hours through Medi-Cal under EPSDT, whether or not they're on a waiver. The prescription and hours justification come from your child's physician; a home health/nursing agency then staffs the hours.

Reality check

Approved hours and staffed hours are not the same thing. California has a serious pediatric home-nursing shortage, and many families have authorized hours no agency can fill. Things that help: registering with multiple agencies, asking your discharge planner which agencies actually staff your ZIP code, considering the HCBA Waiver (whose care management team helps chase staffing, and whose WPCS benefit can pay family members), and documenting every unstaffed shift — that record supports appeals and advocacy.

Equipment, supplies & therapies

Medi-Cal covers durable medical equipment (wheelchairs, standers, hospital beds, suction, oxygen), medical supplies (trach and g-tube supplies, formula, catheters, diapers over age 5 with documentation), and PT/OT/speech. The process runs on a physician's prescription plus a letter of medical necessity, submitted by a Medi-Cal-enrolled supplier through a Treatment Authorization Request (TAR) or your managed care plan's authorization process.

If your child is in a Medi-Cal managed care plan (most kids are), the plan handles authorizations — and its member services line must help you find in-network suppliers. Denials can be appealed, then taken to a state fair hearing, and for managed care, an Independent Medical Review through the Department of Managed Health Care. Under EPSDT, "not a covered benefit" is rarely the end of the story for a child.

Two benefits families miss

Enhanced Care Management (ECM)A free CalAIM benefit through your child's managed care plan: a dedicated care manager for "children with medical complexity" who helps coordinate specialists, authorizations, and community services. Call the number on the Medi-Cal plan card and ask for ECM.
Concurrent hospice careUnder federal law, children on Medi-Cal can receive hospice or palliative care support at home while continuing full curative treatment. Families do not have to "give up" on treatment to get hospice-level nursing, symptom management, and family support. Ask your child's team about pediatric palliative care and concurrent care.
Where to get help

Your county Medi-Cal office handles eligibility; your managed care plan handles services. For denials and appeals, Disability Rights California (1-800-776-5746) publishes excellent self-advocacy guides and runs an intake line. The Health Consumer Alliance (healthconsumer.org) provides free help with Medi-Cal problems statewide.

Programs · The medically fragile waiver

The HCBA Waiver

The Home and Community-Based Alternatives (HCBA) Waiver is California's program for people whose medical needs would otherwise require a nursing facility or hospital — including medically fragile children. If you knew CAP/C in North Carolina, this is the closest California equivalent: care management, home nursing, home modifications, and Medi-Cal based on your child's income alone.

Last reviewed July 2026 — check the DHCS dashboard for current waitlist numbers
What it is Who qualifies Services Paid family caregivers How to apply The assessment If you're denied Turning 21

What is the HCBA Waiver?

It's a Medi-Cal "1915(c) waiver" run by the Department of Health Care Services (DHCS). The idea: your child qualifies for care in a facility — a nursing facility, subacute unit, or hospital — but the state "waives" its usual rules so that the same level of care can be delivered at home instead. Every participant gets a Care Management Team (a nurse and a social worker from a regional Waiver Agency) who builds a plan of treatment, coordinates all services, and checks in monthly.

Three reasons families fight for this waiver
  1. Institutional deeming: only the child's own income/assets count for Medi-Cal — parent income becomes irrelevant.
  2. WPCS: Waiver Personal Care Services hours can be paid to caregivers the family chooses — including parents and other relatives — on top of IHSS.
  3. After 21: the HCBA Waiver is the only Medi-Cal pathway that pays for private duty nursing for adults. For a trach/vent-dependent young adult, being on this waiver is the difference between home and a facility.

Who qualifies

  • Medical level of care: the applicant must need the level of care of a Medi-Cal-funded nursing facility, subacute facility, ICF/DD-Continuous Nursing, or acute hospital. For children this typically means significant daily skilled needs — for example tracheostomy, ventilator or continuous respiratory support, complex tube feeding with high aspiration risk, uncontrolled seizures requiring skilled response, or total care with skilled interventions.
  • Medi-Cal: you must have or apply for Medi-Cal. (If the child doesn't qualify because of parent income, the waiver itself fixes that through institutional deeming.)
  • Any age. There is no upper or lower age limit — but children under 21 get priority for enrollment.
  • Living at home or heading home: the waiver serves people in a community residence, including a family home, or people in a facility who could go home with waiver support.
The waitlist — and why you should apply anyway

The waiver reached capacity in July 2023 and has had a waitlist since. Capacity is expanding each year (14,374 slots in 2026, rising to 16,174 in 2027), and DHCS publishes waitlist numbers monthly on its enrollment dashboard. Three groups get Reserve Capacity priority when slots open:

  1. Applicants under 21,
  2. Applicants who have been in a hospital or health facility for 60+ days,
  3. Applicants transferring from another HCBS waiver that can no longer meet their needs.

A medically fragile child usually fits group 1 (and often group 2 straight from a long admission). The waitlist is real, but for children it moves — and you cannot be prioritized off a list you never joined. Apply now.

What the waiver provides

Care managementA nurse + social worker team that assesses, writes the plan of treatment, coordinates every service below, and troubleshoots monthly. For many families this alone is transformative.
Home nursingSkilled nursing (including shift/private duty nursing) beyond what State Plan Medi-Cal provides — and the only PDN source for participants over 21.
WPCSWaiver Personal Care Services — attendant care hours above the IHSS assessment, payable to providers the family selects, including family members. See below.
RespitePlanned relief for family caregivers, in-home or out-of-home.
Home & vehicle changesEnvironmental accessibility adaptations — ramps, lifts, bathroom modifications — and certain medical equipment/supplies not otherwise covered.
Transition servicesHelp moving from a facility back into the community, plus family training and other supports in the plan of treatment.

Paid family caregivers (WPCS + IHSS)

California pays family caregivers through two stacked programs. IHSS is the base: county-assessed hours for personal care and paramedical tasks, and since February 2024 parents can be paid providers without restrictions. WPCS sits on top for HCBA participants whose assessed needs exceed the IHSS maximum: additional hours, same general provider rules — the family recruits and selects the provider, who enrolls and submits electronic timesheets. Parents, other relatives, and hired caregivers can all serve as WPCS providers.

Together, IHSS + WPCS can fund a substantial portion of round-the-clock care for the most complex children — and because the family chooses providers, it's also the practical answer to the nursing shortage: when no agency nurse is available, a trained, paid family member is the one who keeps the child home. For WPCS questions, DHCS runs a dedicated line at (916) 552-9214; general HCBA questions go to (833) 388-4551 or HCBAlternatives@dhcs.ca.gov.

How to apply

  1. Get the application

    It's a six-page form from DHCS — download the HCBA application (PDF) (also available in Spanish). It asks about diagnoses and daily medical needs, with checkboxes for things like tube feeding, suctioning, oxygen, and ventilator support.

  2. Make the medical need undeniable

    Check every box that applies and use the blank space (or attach pages). Describe a full 24 hours of care: every feed, med pass, suction, repositioning, seizure protocol, and overnight intervention. Attach a physician letter and a current clinic note. Under-described applications get triaged as less urgent.

  3. Flag your priority group

    If your child is under 21, currently hospitalized 60+ days, or transferring from another waiver, write it on the application so it's routed to Reserve Capacity review.

  4. Send it to your Waiver Agency

    Nine regional Waiver Agencies process applications; which one serves you depends on your county — and in Los Angeles and Orange Counties, on your ZIP code. The current agency list and map are on the DHCS HCBA page. A few rural counties have no assigned agency; there, applications go directly to DHCS (address on the form).

  5. Keep proof, then follow up

    Keep a dated copy of everything. Confirm the agency received it, ask for your waitlist status in writing, and update the agency whenever your child is hospitalized (a 60-day stay can change priority) or needs escalate.

Preparing for the assessment

When a slot opens, the Waiver Agency's nurse will assess your child — usually at home. Families consistently give the same advice:

  • Describe the worst days, not the best days. Assessors write down what you say; if you minimize ("we manage okay"), the plan of treatment will assume you're managing okay.
  • Walk through 24 hours out loud, including nights: how often you're up, what happens when equipment alarms, what a seizure or desat response looks like minute by minute.
  • Show the med list, the equipment, and the supply shelf. Volume communicates acuity.
  • Have your ask ready: how many nursing hours, how many WPCS hours, what home modifications, what respite. You are allowed to request specific services.

What if we're denied (or the plan is too small)?

You have appeal rights at every stage. A denial of waiver enrollment, a denial of a requested service, or a reduction in services must come with a written Notice of Action explaining how to request a state fair hearing — request it within the deadline on the notice (and if services are being cut, request it quickly; filing before the cut-off date usually keeps services in place pending the hearing, a protection called "aid paid pending").

  • Ask the Waiver Agency to explain exactly which criterion wasn't met — often it's a documentation gap you can fix and reapply.
  • Get the physician to write directly to the disputed point (e.g., "requires skilled nursing intervention during sleep hours because…").
  • Disability Rights California (1-800-776-5746) publishes HCBA self-advocacy materials and can advise on hearings.

What happens at 21

Deadline warning

Under-21 priority doesn't last forever. A young adult living in the community who is not fully enrolled roughly six months after their 21st birthday loses priority status on the waitlist — and once over 21, there is no other Medi-Cal source for home private-duty nursing. If your child is 17–20 and not yet on the waiver, treat the application as urgent, and see our transition guide for the full 18–21 checklist.

Programs · The Lanterman entitlement

Regional Centers & the DD Waiver

California made a promise no other state has made: the Lanterman Act entitles people with developmental disabilities to the services they need to live in the community — for life, at no cost to the family, with no enrollment cap. The front door is your local Regional Center.

Last reviewed July 2026 — service specifics vary by Regional Center
What they are Babies: Early Start Eligibility at 3+ Services & the IPP The DD Waiver Self-Determination If you're denied

What Regional Centers are

Twenty-one nonprofit Regional Centers contract with the state Department of Developmental Services (DDS) to assess, coordinate, and purchase services for Californians with developmental disabilities. Once eligible, your child gets a service coordinator and an individualized plan, and the Regional Center pays for needed services that no other program covers. Find your center by county at dds.ca.gov/rc.

Babies and toddlers: Early Start (0–3)

For children under 36 months, eligibility is deliberately generous: a significant developmental delay, an established risk condition (many genetic diagnoses, prematurity-related conditions, and neurological injuries qualify automatically), or high risk of delay. Early Start provides in-home infant development services, PT/OT/speech, and family support. Anyone can refer — including a parent. Call your Regional Center and say "I'd like an Early Start evaluation"; timelines for evaluation are set in law, so note the date you call.

NICU families

Ask for the Early Start referral before discharge. Prematurity with complications, HIE, brain bleeds, and most syndromes qualify as established risk — you don't have to wait for a delay to show up on a chart.

Eligibility from age 3 on (Lanterman)

After age 3, ongoing eligibility requires a developmental disability that began before age 18, is expected to continue indefinitely, and is a substantial disability. Qualifying categories:

  • Intellectual disability
  • Cerebral palsy
  • Epilepsy
  • Autism
  • A "fifth category" condition closely related to intellectual disability or requiring similar treatment — where many complex genetic and neurological conditions land

Many medically fragile children qualify under CP, epilepsy, or the fifth category even when cognition can't yet be assessed. The Regional Center performs (and pays for) the evaluations.

Services and the IPP

Services are written into an Individual Program Plan (IPP) (or IFSP for Early Start), rebuilt with the family at least every three years and reviewable any time needs change. Common purchases for medically fragile kids:

RespiteIn-home or out-of-home relief hours for family caregivers — often the first and most-used service.
Day & behavioral servicesInfant programs, ABA and behavior support, social/recreation services, camps.
Home & equipment helpCertain home modifications, specialized equipment and supplies not covered elsewhere.
Nursing-adjacent supportSome Regional Centers authorize specialized supervision or nursing-supported services when generic programs can't meet medical needs — and have bridged private duty nursing for young adults stuck on the HCBA waitlist.
Family supportsParent training, sibling support, transportation, and coordination with schools and health plans.

Regional Centers are the payer of last resort — they'll ask you to use Medi-Cal, private insurance, and school services first, and may ask you to apply to those programs. That's normal and legal; what they cannot do is refuse to meet an IPP need simply because it's expensive.

The HCBS-DD Waiver — the no-waitlist Medi-Cal door

Key fact

Regional Center clients can enroll in the HCBS-DD Waiver, which has no enrollment cap and no waitlist. Like the HCBA Waiver, it brings institutional deeming: your child can get full-scope Medi-Cal counting only their own income — often years before an HCBA slot opens. If your child is Regional Center-eligible and doesn't have Medi-Cal because of your income, ask the service coordinator about DD Waiver enrollment for institutional deeming. If your child's needs are primarily skilled-nursing medical, the HCBA Waiver may still be the better fit long-term (DHCS publishes a comparison of the two), and children can move between waivers as needs dictate.

The Self-Determination Program

Instead of services purchased from Regional Center vendors, the Self-Determination Program (SDP) gives families an individual budget and the freedom to build their own supports — hiring workers directly, choosing nontraditional providers, and moving money between needs. It's more work (there's an orientation, a spending plan, and a fiscal intermediary) and more power. Families with unusual schedules, rural addresses, or hard-to-staff needs often find SDP is what finally makes services real. Ask your service coordinator about SDP enrollment.

If you're denied — or services are cut

Everything a Regional Center decides comes with due-process rights under the Lanterman Act. If eligibility or a service is denied, you're entitled to a written notice and can file for a fair hearing (an independent administrative judge). Deadlines are short — act on the notice immediately, and if an existing service is being reduced, file before the effective date so the service continues pending the hearing.

  • First, request an informal meeting with the Regional Center — many disputes settle there.
  • Each Regional Center has a Clients' Rights Advocate (from Disability Rights California) whose job is to help you — free. Ask reception for the CRA's contact.
  • For fifth-category and autism eligibility disputes, an outside neuropsychological or medical letter directly addressing the legal criteria is often decisive.
Programs · Specialty care to 21

California Children's Services (CCS)

CCS is a statewide program — older than Medi-Cal itself — that arranges and pays for specialty medical care for children and young adults under 21 with eligible serious conditions. It sets pediatric quality standards ("paneled" providers and approved specialty centers) and runs physical and occupational therapy inside public schools.

Eligible conditions Financial rules Whole Child Model Medical Therapy Program How to get in

Which conditions qualify

CCS covers conditions that are physically disabling or require treatment with medicine, surgery, or rehabilitation — a long regulatory list that includes, among many others:

  • Congenital heart disease and other major congenital anomalies
  • Neurological conditions: cerebral palsy, uncontrolled seizure disorders, severe brain/spinal cord injury, neuromuscular disease
  • Cancer, sickle cell disease, hemophilia and serious blood disorders
  • Chronic kidney, liver, intestinal, and lung disease; cystic fibrosis
  • Metabolic and endocrine disorders (including many newborn-screen diagnoses)
  • Cleft lip/palate, severe scoliosis and orthopedic conditions, severe burns, severe hearing loss
  • Prematurity-related conditions requiring intensive care (including NICU care at CCS-approved units) and High-Risk Infant Follow-Up afterward

If your child is medically fragile enough to be reading this site, odds are high at least one diagnosis is CCS-eligible. The county CCS medical team makes the eligibility decision from records — you don't have to prove it yourself.

The financial rules (gentler than you'd think)

AutomaticChildren with full-scope Medi-Cal are financially eligible automatically.
Income testOtherwise, family adjusted gross income of $40,000 or less qualifies — or any income if expected out-of-pocket costs for the child's CCS condition exceed 20% of family income (easy to meet in a bad year).
No test at allDiagnostic evaluations to determine eligibility, the school-based Medical Therapy Program, and High-Risk Infant Follow-Up have no financial eligibility requirement. Every family qualifies for these.

Classic CCS vs. the Whole Child Model

How CCS is delivered depends on your county. In "classic" counties, the county CCS office (with state oversight) authorizes and case-manages CCS services directly, separate from your Medi-Cal plan. In Whole Child Model (WCM) counties, the Medi-Cal managed care plan administers CCS benefits inside the plan — one card, one authorization system, one care coordination team. WCM launched in 21 counties in 2018–19 (including Orange, Monterey, Santa Barbara, San Mateo, and much of Northern California) and expanded to a dozen more counties in January 2025.

What this means for you

In a WCM county, direct CCS questions and authorization problems to your child's Medi-Cal plan's pediatric/CCS unit — and know that CCS standards still apply: care must come from CCS-paneled providers and approved specialty centers, and plans must maintain CCS-level case management. In a classic county, your county CCS office remains the authorizer, and CCS operates alongside (not inside) the health plan. Either way, the state CCS line at (888) 452-8609 can point you to the right office.

The Medical Therapy Program (MTP)

One of California's best-kept secrets: CCS operates Medical Therapy Units inside public schools, providing physical and occupational therapy, and equipment clinics, for children with qualifying neuromuscular and orthopedic conditions (cerebral palsy prominently among them). MTP has no income test, works alongside school-based IEP therapies rather than replacing them, and its therapists are often the most experienced pediatric neuro therapists in a county. Referral goes through the county CCS office.

How to get in

  1. Anyone refers

    Doctors, hospitals, schools, and parents themselves can refer a child to the county CCS office. NICUs and children's hospitals often start the referral automatically — ask if one was opened.

  2. County checks residence & finances, medical team checks the diagnosis

    Send requested records promptly; eligibility is determined from documentation. Diagnostic services can be authorized before eligibility is final when a CCS condition is suspected.

  3. Use CCS-paneled providers

    Once open, care for the CCS condition must come from CCS-paneled providers and CCS-approved specialty centers — which in practice means the major children's hospitals and pediatric specialty groups. Ask any new specialist "are you CCS-paneled?"

  4. Renew annually

    CCS eligibility is reviewed yearly (medical + financial). Calendar it, and tell CCS about new diagnoses — coverage follows the conditions CCS has on file.

CCS + Medi-Cal + private insurance

These stack. Private insurance bills first, CCS covers condition-related care within its scope, Medi-Cal (with EPSDT) fills what's left. Families with all three should route condition-related authorizations through CCS/WCM, and use EPSDT appeals for anything the others deny. And when it works well, CCS case management is the closest thing California has to a quarterback for a complex child's specialty care — use it.

Programs · Paid caregiving

IHSS: In-Home Supportive Services

IHSS pays a caregiver, by the hour, for the help your child needs to live safely at home — personal care, paramedical tasks, and protective supervision. Since February 2024, that caregiver can simply be you: California eliminated the old restrictions on parents being paid providers for their own children.

What IHSS pays for (for children)

Your child needs full-scope Medi-Cal and a documented functional need for help beyond what a typical child the same age requires. For minors, payable services include:

Personal careBathing, dressing, feeding assistance, toileting/incontinence care, transfers and positioning, help with mobility.
Paramedical servicesCare that would otherwise be done by medical staff, delegated by the child's doctor: g-tube feeds and site care, suctioning, medication administration, catheterization, ostomy care, range-of-motion. Documented on a physician-signed form.
Protective supervision24-hour-need category for children who, because of cognitive or neurological impairment, are unaware of danger and would hurt themselves without constant observation. This is the single largest hour category — and the most commonly under-claimed for kids with severe neurological impairment. It requires a physician form (SOC 821) and evidence of self-injurious/unaware-of-danger behavior.
AccompanimentTime accompanying the child to medical appointments.

Note: some adult IHSS services (domestic chores, meal prep as a standalone task, yard work) aren't payable for minors — the program pays for the child's extra needs, not general parenting or housekeeping.

Parents as paid providers — the 2024 change

Key fact

Effective February 19, 2024, California law eliminated the old parent-provider restrictions for minor recipients. Parents no longer have to prove they left full-time work, that no other provider exists, or that the other parent is unavailable. A minor now has the same free choice of provider as an adult recipient: either parent, both parents splitting hours, a grandparent, or a hired caregiver — any enrolled provider works. Parents who can't be providers themselves (for example, due to work-authorization status) may hire a non-parent provider for the child's hours.

  • Provider enrollment still applies: the parent completes the county provider paperwork, a DOJ/FBI background check, and orientation.
  • Program bucket matters: one IHSS sub-program (PCSP) still can't pay parents under federal rules — counties should place or transfer children who want a parent provider into a different sub-program (like CFCO). If you're told "parents can't be paid," ask the county to transfer the case to a program that allows a parent provider — that is current policy.
  • Taxes: live-in family providers can generally exclude IHSS wages from federal income tax under IRS Notice 2014-7 — worth reading with a tax preparer.

How to apply

  1. Call or apply to your county IHSS office

    Every county has one (search "[your county] IHSS apply" or find it via CDSS). You'll complete an application for the child and provide Medi-Cal information.

  2. Get the medical forms signed

    The county sends a health-care certification (SOC 873) to be signed by the child's doctor; ask the doctor to be specific and maximal about needs. For protective supervision, also request the SOC 821 and, ideally, a physician letter describing hazard-unawareness with examples.

  3. The home assessment

    A county social worker visits, observes, and interviews you (see below), then authorizes monthly hours by task.

  4. Enroll the provider(s)

    Whoever will be paid — you included — completes provider enrollment: SOC 426 forms, Live Scan background check, orientation, and direct deposit/timesheet setup. Hours are logged in the state's Electronic Services Portal twice monthly.

Surviving the assessment

  • Compare to a typical child of the same age, task by task. IHSS pays only for the extra — so spell out what a typical 7-year-old does alone that yours cannot.
  • Narrate the hardest 24 hours, night wakings and all. Keep a one-week care diary beforehand; hand the social worker a copy.
  • Demonstrate, don't summarize. "Feeding takes 45 minutes and she aspirates without pacing" beats "feeding is hard."
  • Don't tidy the medical reality away. Assessors can only count what they see and hear.

Hours and pay

Hours are authorized per month based on assessed need, up to 283 hours/month for severely impaired recipients (195 for non-severely impaired). Wages are set per county — every county now pays above the state minimum wage, and providers accrue sick leave; overtime is possible within workweek limits. The county issues a Notice of Action listing hours by task — read it line by line, because each undercounted task is appealable.

Appeals

If hours are denied or lower than the real need, request a state fair hearing within 90 days of the Notice of Action (file before the effective date to keep current hours during the appeal). Bring the care diary, physician letters, and task-by-task timing. Many families win significant hour increases on appeal — especially protective supervision. Disability Rights California publishes step-by-step IHSS hearing guides.

Resources · California-specific

Backup power for equipment-dependent kids

If your child runs on a ventilator, oxygen concentrator, feeding pump, or suction, a California power outage — wildfire shutoff, heat wave, earthquake — is a medical emergency. There's no single program that hands you a generator; families build a safety net from six pieces. Here's the order.

Last reviewed July 2026 — utility programs change every year; confirm with your utility

  1. Enroll in Medical Baseline

    Every major utility (PG&E, SCE, SDG&E, and municipal utilities like LADWP and SMUD) runs a Medical Baseline program for households with medical equipment: extra energy at the lowest rate, and — critically — extra advance notification before a Public Safety Power Shutoff (PSPS). It's a short form your child's doctor signs. This is step one because the battery and generator programs below usually require it.

  2. Ask about free battery programs

    Utilities run no-cost portable battery programs for medically vulnerable customers in fire-threat areas — PG&E's Portable Battery Program and Backup Power Transfer Meter offering, SCE's Critical Care Backup Battery program, and SDG&E's battery/generator assistance offerings are the current examples. Eligibility usually keys off Medical Baseline enrollment plus location. Call your utility's Medical Baseline line and ask specifically: "What backup battery or generator programs is my child eligible for?"

  3. Pursue generator/battery funding

    Where utility programs fall short: utility rebates for generators and transfer meters in high-fire-threat districts, Regional Center IPP purchases (safety equipment tied to the disability), and nonprofit grants can each fund equipment. Get the prescription in writing — a physician letter of medical necessity for backup power for life-sustaining equipment strengthens every one of these requests.

  4. Use the waivers

    For children on the HCBA Waiver or Regional Center services, raise backup power at the plan-of-treatment/IPP meeting: environmental adaptations and specialized equipment categories have covered batteries and related home wiring where justified as necessary for safe home care. Not guaranteed — but never funded if never requested.

  5. Appeal insurance denials

    Health plans routinely deny generators as "not medical equipment." Appeals succeed most often for battery systems integral to prescribed equipment (e.g., ventilator batteries, extended battery packs) — pursue internal appeal, then Independent Medical Review through DMHC for managed care. Ask the DME company what battery options exist for each life-support device your child uses; prescribed device batteries are the cleanest coverage path.

  6. Build the emergency plan

    Register at Smart911 (so dispatchers see your child's equipment), ask your fire department about local medically-fragile registries, plan an evacuation destination with power (family, hotel list, hospital as last resort), keep a go-bag with 72 hours of supplies, charged batteries, and copies of medical documents, and know your equipment's real battery runtimes. Practice once before fire season.

During an outage

If battery reserves are failing and you cannot relocate, call 911 — do not wait until the last hour of charge. Notify your utility that a medically fragile child is in the home (Medical Baseline flags help, but a call creates a record), and your DME company for emergency battery swaps.

Resources · Ages 14–22

What happens when they're older?

Between 18 and 21, several of the programs holding your child's care together change or end — on the state's schedule, not yours. Families who start at 16 glide; families who start at 20 scramble. Here's the timeline.

Last reviewed July 2026

The timeline

Age 14–16School transition planning begins in the IEP (legally by 16). Regional Center IPPs add transition goals. Start the conversation with specialists about which adult providers will take over.
Age 17If not on the HCBA Waiver, apply now — under-21 priority takes time to convert into enrollment, and it expires (below). Research decision-making support options before the 18th birthday.
Age 18Legal adulthood: parents lose automatic medical decision authority. Choose supported decision-making, health-care directives/POA, or (where truly needed) limited conservatorship — California law now favors less-restrictive options first. SSI: at 18, only the young adult's own income counts, so many previously-ineligible kids qualify — apply the month they turn 18; SSI brings Medi-Cal with it. Existing childhood SSI cases get an adult-rules redetermination. IHSS continues (adult rules; parents can remain paid providers). Register for selective service if applicable; ABLE accounts become a useful savings tool.
Age 20Final HCBA push if not yet enrolled. Line up adult primary care and specialists (many pediatric clinics extend to 21–25 for complex patients — ask). Confirm which services in the IPP continue into adult day/program models.
Age 21CCS ends. For certain CCS conditions (cystic fibrosis, hemophilia, sickle cell, certain metabolic/genetic conditions and more), the Genetically Handicapped Persons Program (GHPP) continues specialty coverage into adulthood — apply before the birthday so coverage is seamless. School services end (through the school year the student turns 22 in California). HCBA: a young adult in the community not fully enrolled by ~6 months after turning 21 loses waitlist priority — and HCBA is the only adult Medi-Cal source of home private-duty nursing.
LifelongRegional Center services are a lifetime entitlement — the IPP evolves into adult supports (day programs, supported living, SDP budgets). Medi-Cal continues under adult rules; IHSS continues; palliative care and hospice remain available with adult concurrent-care options varying by plan.
The two unforgiving deadlines

(1) HCBA enrollment by ~21½ for anyone who will ever need home nursing as an adult. (2) GHPP application before the 21st birthday for CCS kids with GHPP-eligible conditions. Miss these and there is no easy repair.

Decision-making support, briefly

California recognizes supported decision-making agreements as an alternative to conservatorship, and courts must consider less-restrictive options before granting one. For a young adult who can express preferences, SDM + a health-care directive + release-of-information forms often covers what families actually need. Disability Rights California and your Regional Center's Clients' Rights Advocate can walk you through the choices — before the 18th birthday, not after.

Every program on this site exists because families made noise. Waitlists shrink, rules improve, and paid-parent restrictions fall when legislators hear real stories from real districts.

Our advocacy priorities

  1. End the HCBA waitlist. A waiver for medically fragile people should not have a multi-year line. We support continued capacity expansion until enrollment is timely for every eligible child and adult.
  2. Fix the pediatric home-nursing shortage. Rates and workforce policy that make children's shifts staffable — so authorized hours become actual nurses at actual bedsides.
  3. Protect paid family caregiving. Defend the 2024 IHSS parent-provider reforms and expand WPCS access, so the people already doing the care can afford to keep doing it.
  4. Smooth the 21 cliff. Automatic GHPP handoffs from CCS, and HCBA enrollment guarantees for aging-out youth who need home nursing.
  5. One front door. A single statewide point of entry that screens families for Medi-Cal, Regional Center, CCS, IHSS, and HCBA together — the map, built into the system itself.

Write your legislators

Find your state Assembly member and Senator at findyourrep.legislature.ca.gov. Three paragraphs is enough: who your child is, one concrete system failure (with dates and numbers), and one specific ask from the list above. Personal stories from constituents move votes more than any statistic.

Share your story

With your consent, we feature California families on this site and connect stories to journalists and policymakers when it can help. You control what's shared and can withdraw at any time. Contact us with "Our story" in the subject.
